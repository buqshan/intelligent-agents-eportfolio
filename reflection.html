Section 07

Reflective Commentary

Structured with Rolfe, Freshwater and Jasper's (2001) What? So what? Now what? framework.

What?

Across ten weeks I moved from answering questions about agents to building and deploying one. My successes, the 88% Unit 5 score and a green test suite, taught me less than four moments of friction. Peers told me my telecom agent was dangerously autonomous. A word count rose while I cut words. My deployed app crashed with HTTP 500 the day I thought it was finished. And, preparing this portfolio, I found that my Planner sends "core router" incidents down a BGP path. Following Rolfe, Freshwater and Jasper (2001), I treat these as the critical incidents of the module.

So what?

Receiving challenge to my expertise. I wrote the forum post with the confidence of two decades in UAE telecom operations. When Luis and Yasmin raised misalignment and the risk of a wrong reboot, my first draft reply to Luis admitted that my post had omitted these risks, and I asked for it to be rephrased "without criticizing my initial post". Looking back, that request was defensive: I was protecting professional identity rather than engaging with the argument. Their points proved more durable than mine. In week 11, I deliberately designed agents that recommend rather than act, which is the clearest evidence that my thinking changed rather than merely my wording.

Frustration and verification in the team. Anne's seven-point review of v9 was initially daunting, because it meant cutting the draft by more than half. Then, across v15–v18, the reported count kept rising as I removed words. I was exasperated, and I said so. The cause was the AI tool counting diagram labels inconsistently; the true prose count was 1,053. The lesson was not about word counts. I had followed an instrument's output for hours without questioning the instrument. Our group moved through something like Tuckman's (1965) storming into norming once Anne proposed a fixed word allocation. My most useful contribution was integration under constraint: verifying each review point, restructuring, and pressing teammates for drafts so the deadline held. My weakest moment was final review. I raised the accessed dates but let the model-version inconsistency pass, and a reviewer who sees a problem and stays silent has not finished the job. I also learned from Fedi's candour. His first draft stated openly that he had not yet studied the relevant unit and would "switch terms" if the team needed it. That openness made his section easier to integrate than a confident but brittle draft would have been, and I am trying to adopt the habit.

Anxiety, cost and deployment. In week 11 my strongest emotion was anxiety that the tutor would click a dead link. That worry drove me through the Colab proxy, ngrok authentication failures, a costed AWS option I rejected over overspend risk, a plan to pay for ngrok, and finally Streamlit Community Cloud. The relief when the public URL worked was real, but the episode showed that I had treated deployment as the last step rather than a design requirement. The design proposal had named Streamlit from the start.

Using generative AI honestly. I relied heavily on Claude for code, slides and drafts of peer reviews. Its strengths were speed and explanation; asking why each cell worked built my understanding. Its weaknesses shaped my learning more. The presentation was drafted before the code existed, and the code was then written to match it, so test counts and narratives appeared before evidence. One placeholder citation (arXiv:2502.XXXXX) was invented. I refused to guess and traced the real source to Nature Communications. Gerlich's (2025) warning about cognitive offloading, which I had critiqued in a peer's post for relying on self-report, described my own behaviour more accurately than I would like.

Connecting to theory. Three module ideas explained my own conduct. BDI's tension between commitment and reconsideration (Bratman, Israel and Pollack, 1988) described my hosting decisions: I over-committed to ngrok, then reconsidered when the environment changed. PEAS made me notice that my test suite's performance measure was "output has the right keys", not "diagnosis is correct". I had optimised a proxy. Brooks's (1991) argument for testing in the real world matched my experience: the faults that mattered appeared only on deployment.

Now what?

What produced my learning was not reading alone but contradiction: each incident set something I had claimed beside evidence that disagreed with it. A peer's scenario contradicted my autonomy claim. A manual count contradicted a tool's count. A crash contradicted "finished", and a two-line probe contradicted 26 passing tests. I will therefore change five actions.

  • Evidence first. No claim enters a slide, report or status update without an artefact behind it (a log line, a test ID, a screenshot). I am applying this now to the RAID logs and status reports in my project-governance work.
  • Adversarial testing by someone else. Rules and their tests will not share an author, and I will probe classifiers with boundary inputs before trusting a pass rate.
  • AI as reviewer, not author, for anything I must defend. I will write the first version, use AI to critique it, and keep a short log of AI use for disclosure under UoEO guidance.
  • Bounded autonomy by default. NOC automation I propose will separate recommending from acting, with approval gates and a kill switch for irreversible actions.
  • Complete reviews. At final review I will report every inconsistency I find, even when raising it is socially awkward.

The module began with Wooldridge's (2009) definition of an agent as autonomous action in pursuit of design objectives. I end it convinced that the harder engineering problem is specifying, bounding and evidencing those objectives, and that the same is true of my own practice as a developer.
