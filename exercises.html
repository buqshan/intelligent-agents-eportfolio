Section 02

Exercises and Theory

Reactive and hybrid agents (Unit 5)

After five progressive question sets I sat a 40-question exam and scored 88%. A coverage check exposed gaps in abstract agent formalism, environment properties and the IRMA architecture, which I then targeted (Wooldridge, 2009). The ideas that stayed with me were Brooks's (1991) claim that the world is its own best model and BDI's tension between commitment and reconsideration (Bratman, Israel and Pollack, 1988).

First-order logic

My errors were more instructive than my correct answers. I accepted f(x) as a well-formed formula, classified Employee(x) as a function and missed the type error in P(P(x)): all the same confusion between terms, which denote individuals, and formulas, which denote truth values. I needed a step-by-step walkthrough to see why ∀ pairs with → and ∃ with ∧, and why the agent variable depends on the service variable.

# Exercise A2: "Every active service is monitored by at least one agent"
∀x ((Service(x) ∧ Active(x)) → ∃y (Agent(y) ∧ Monitors(y, x)))

# Exercise A3: "No agent can invoke a tool that is not registered"
¬∃x ∃t (Agent(x) ∧ Tool(t) ∧ CanInvoke(x,t) ∧ ¬Registered(t))
≡ ∀x ∀t ((Agent(x) ∧ Tool(t) ∧ CanInvoke(x,t)) → Registered(t))

Exercise A3 is effectively a guardrail written in logic. It changed how I read tool-permission policies, and I reuse the pattern in Case Study 1. Reading Searle (1969) on speech acts explained the basis of agent communication languages such as KQML (Finin et al., 1994), which our team later decided we did not need.
